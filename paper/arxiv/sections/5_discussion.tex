\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:disc}

\noindent\textbf{Why the coupling does not pay.}
The path-aware cost replaces $d(p^r,p_\tau)$ by the arrival time
$A^{\mathcal{B}}(p^r,p_\tau,t_0)$. The two differ only by the waits and
detours that reservations impose on the empty trip to the pickup. In our
layouts that difference is small on two counts. First, the empty trip is a
minor part of a task: under batch load with 32 robots, service time is
282--287 ticks of which 216--220 are queueing before assignment
(\cref{tab:main}); the remaining 66 ticks or so contain the trip to the
pickup, two dwells and the loaded trip, and the pickup leg is the shorter one because
robots start near the aisles. Second, with 2-wide aisles the planner absorbs
most interactions by a one-tick wait or a lane change: a run of 200 tasks
executes 274 wait actions against 14.9 thousand moves, about 1.4 waits per
task. The arrival-time estimate therefore rarely re-orders candidates, and
when it does the change is within the noise of task sampling. Congestion that
does matter concentrates at the docks, which the pickup-leg cost cannot see
and which the endpoint capacity already regulates. The negative result is
thus a statement about two-lane grid warehouses with well-formed endpoints,
not about coupling in general; narrow one-lane aisles, non-uniform demand or
long empty trips would change the balance, and the code supports such
layouts.

\noindent\textbf{Cost analysis.}
The whole stack runs at 0.02--1.0 ms of planner time per simulated tick for 4
to 48 robots on one core (\cref{fig:scaling}); a tick of one second of robot
motion therefore leaves three orders of magnitude of headroom, and the
coupling's extra A* calls ($+35\%$ at 48 robots) are affordable even though
they are not useful here. Windowed CBS at the small fleet sizes where it is
exact costs 0.10--0.32 ms/tick. Memory is dominated by the cached distance
tables, one $|V|$-vector per endpoint.

\noindent\textbf{What made the loop robust.}
During development, each of three mechanisms removed a jam we had observed;
they are design history, not ablations in the committed benchmark. (i) The hold
cascade (\cref{alg:pp}) is what makes ``no path found'' safe: without
re-planning the robots that cross a held cell, a hold silently invalidates
earlier reservations. (ii) Endpoint capacities bound the queue in front of a
dock; with unbounded queues, robots waiting for the same dock filled both
lanes of the corridor and the robot leaving the dock could not get out.
(iii) Planning held robots first, across planning groups, resolves the one
gridlock that survived (i) and (ii): a robot that had just delivered at a
corner dock parks on the dock's single access cell, is boxed in by the robots
queuing for that dock, fails to plan, and---because parking robots are
planned last---never gets the priority it needs to leave. These are
engineering facts about prioritized lifelong planning that the literature
tends to summarise as ``we did not observe deadlocks''; we report them
because each one was found by a jam in our own runs before it was fixed.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations.}
Everything in this paper is simulation on a unit-speed 4-connected grid with
synchronous ticks: no kinematics, no localisation error, no communication
delay, no real robots. Tasks are sampled uniformly over endpoints; real
demand is skewed and time-varying, which would change both the value of
coupling and the dock bottleneck. The battery model is linear and the
charging policy is a fixed hysteresis; we do not optimise charging
schedules. Five seeds per configuration bound our resolution to differences
of a few percent, which is enough to rule out large gains from the coupling
but not to rank the close variants. CBS is only compared where basic CBS is
tractable; symmetry reasoning \citep{li2019symmetry} or bounded-suboptimal
search \citep{li2021eecbs} would extend it to larger groups. Prioritized
planning with a hold cascade is safe but not complete: a robot can be
delayed for several epochs, and although every run finished, we have no proof
that none can deadlock or livelock, and the charging test of
\cref{eq:feasible} is a heuristic, so depletion is likewise excluded only
empirically. The safety, progress and charge results rest on five seeds per
configuration; a larger multi-seed stress test is future work. Finally, the console integration displays the simulator; it
is not connected to a fleet.
