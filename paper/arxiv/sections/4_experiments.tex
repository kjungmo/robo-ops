\section{Experiments}\label{sec:exp}

We ask three questions. (Q1) Is the stack correct and stable in lifelong
operation---no conflicts, no depletions, no deadlocks---across layouts, fleet
sizes and load regimes? (Q2) Does coupling allocation to the planner through
path-aware costs improve throughput or service time over static costs?
(Q3) Which of the remaining design choices (planner backend, window,
replanning period, endpoint capacity, battery term) matter, and what do they
cost in planner time? A second round of experiments, run after an adversarial
review of the first, adds a 60-seed stress sweep for Q1, an ablation of the
hold cascade and of the planning priorities, token-passing baselines, and
four regimes chosen to favour the coupling for Q2
(\cref{subsec:cascade,subsec:baselines,subsec:coupling}).

\subsection{Experimental Setup}\label{subsec:setup}

\noindent\textbf{Layouts.}
\emph{warehouse} ($52\times19$, 715 free cells) has four blocks of five
10-cell shelf lines with 2-wide aisles, 100 pickup points, 9 docks, 4 charger
stations of 2 slots and 48 home bays. \emph{console} ($36\times25$, 658 free
cells) is generated from the zone/line/dock counts that appear in the
operator console's wireframe labels (zones A--D with 3, 3, 2 and 4 shelf
lines; two named shipping docks, modelled as bays of three delivery cells), 84
pickups, 6 docks, 3 stations, 32 bays. \emph{small} ($16\times10$, 104 free
cells) is an open floor with 4 pickups, 2 docks, 2 slots and 8 bays used for
the planner comparison. \emph{narrow} ($52\times15$, 496 free cells) repeats
the warehouse pattern with one-lane aisles: four blocks of four shelf lines
whose single aisle row is closed by shelves on both sides, so robots cannot
pass inside an aisle and a robot loading at a pickup blocks it; 80 pickups, 6
docks, 8 slots, 32 bays. \Cref{tab:layouts} in the appendix lists all counts.

\noindent\textbf{Tasks and load.}
Each run delivers 200 tasks (100 on \emph{small}) whose pickup and dock are
sampled uniformly. Two load regimes are used: a Poisson stream of
$\lambda=0.15$ tasks per tick, which is below the capacity of fleets of 16 or
more robots and saturates a fleet of 8, and \emph{batch}, which releases all
tasks at $t=0$ and measures pure throughput. Loading and unloading dwell is
$\delta=2$ ticks. Initial charge is uniform in $[0.5,1.0]$, except in the
battery stress where it is uniform in $[0.2,0.4]$ so that every robot has to
charge during the run. The stress sweep adds a harder battery regime, initial
charge in $[0.2,0.4]$ with doubled drain ($\beta_{\mathrm{m}}=0.002$,
$\beta_{\mathrm{w}}=0.0004$). The coupling regimes use two non-uniform demand
models: \emph{hotspot}, where 80\% of pickups are drawn from the 10\% of
pickup points nearest the floor centre, and \emph{far}, where pickups are
drawn only from the 25\% of pickup points farthest from any dock. All
parameters are listed in \cref{tab:params}.

\noindent\textbf{Methods.}
Four allocation cost models share the same planner, charging policy and
endpoint capacities: \emph{Greedy} (cheapest pair first, static distance),
\emph{Hungarian} (optimal assignment, static distance), \emph{Proxy}
(Hungarian on distance plus reservation-table congestion, \cref{eq:cost}) and
\emph{\sname} (Hungarian on windowed A* arrival times). The planner backend
is prioritized planning (PP) with the hold cascade unless stated; windowed
CBS is compared on \emph{small} and, in the stress sweep, on every layout.
Defaults are $w=20$, $h=5$, $K=6$, $\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}=3$,
$\kappa_{\mathrm{p}}=1$, $w_{\mathrm{b}}=1$. As baselines we re-implement
token passing (TP) and token passing with task swaps (TPTS)
\citep{ma2017lifelong} in the same simulator, with the same battery model,
dwell, charging policy and feasibility test (\cref{subsec:baselines}).

\noindent\textbf{Metrics.}
Throughput is delivered tasks per 100 ticks; service time is delivery minus
arrival; wait time is first assignment minus arrival; wait actions count
ticks a robot with a goal did not move; holds count planner fall-backs; planner
ms/tick is wall-clock time of allocation plus planning divided by simulated
ticks; conflicts are counted on the executed step (vertex and swap) from the
robots' cells, independently of the reservation table, both by the simulator
and by the trajectory auditor (\cref{subsec:planning}), which also reports
illegal moves, the largest trip delay $W$ and the longest slot wait $Q$ of
\cref{prop:charge}; a run is flagged \emph{stalled} (``deadlocked'' in the
code) when no task is picked up or delivered for 1000 consecutive ticks
while tasks are outstanding, and a run that neither finishes nor stalls stops
at 20{,}000 ticks with its unfinished tasks counted. The first-round sweeps
use seeds 1--5, the stress sweep seeds 1--60 and the other second-round
sweeps seeds 1--30; results are reported as mean\stdv{std}. Runs with the
same seed see the same task stream and initial charge, so we compare
variants by seed-paired relative differences with two-sided 95\%
Student-$t$ intervals ($n-1$ degrees of freedom; \cref{tab:paired} in the
appendix for the first round).

\noindent\textbf{Hardware and protocol.}
All runs are single-threaded Node.js~22 on an AMD Ryzen~5 5500GT. The
first-round sweeps (420 runs) run in one process in 109\,s of wall-clock
time; the second-round sweeps (6{,}180 runs) run on 10 worker processes in
967\,s, so their planner times are inflated by contention and we quote them
only as ratios within one sweep. Runs are deterministic given the seed:
re-running the first round at the final code reproduces every non-timing
field of every run. The four second-round sweeps, including the coupling
regimes, are defined in commit \texttt{4a72ff1} of the repository, which
precedes every commit of their results; later commits changed only what the
auditor and the simulator record, not the sweeps or the simulated behaviour.
The history shows the order of commits, not that no run preceded the first
one. The benchmark
driver, the per-run JSON records and the summary CSVs are committed with the
code.

\subsection{Main Results}\label{subsec:main}
\input{tables/main}

\noindent\textbf{Correctness and stability (Q1).}
Over the 420 first-round runs---8{,}833{,}050 robot-ticks, 5{,}317{,}603
move actions and 81{,}000 delivered tasks---the executed steps contain zero
vertex or swap conflicts, as \cref{cor:exec} requires, according to both the
simulator and the independent auditor, no robot ever depletes, and every run
delivers all of its tasks without tripping the stall detector, on every
layout, fleet size from 4 to 48, load regime, allocation method and planner
backend. Hold fall-backs are rare (at most 5.2 per run on average in the
main sweep, reached with 32 robots under batch load). The battery stress
(\cref{tab:battery}) forces 22.6--23.2 charging sessions per run for 16
robots and still records zero depletions, with a final mean charge of
0.34--0.38.

\input{tables/stress}
The stress sweep (\cref{tab:stress}) repeats this check on 60 seeds for every
layout including \emph{narrow}, two or three fleet sizes per layout, both
planner backends, both load regimes and both battery regimes: 4{,}320 runs
and 51.6 million robot-steps. The auditor finds no vertex conflict, no swap
conflict and no illegal move in any of them. With normal batteries (2{,}160
runs) no robot depletes, no run stalls and every task is delivered; the
longest interval without a pickup or delivery is 126 ticks and the lowest
charge any robot reaches is 0.044. Under doubled drain the picture changes:
robots deplete in 1{,}476 of 2{,}160 runs (9{,}535 depletion events), and
227 runs stall with 510 tasks undelivered, every one of them after a
depletion, because a stranded robot is a permanent obstacle. The depletions
are not planner failures but the charging policy running out of slots: a
robot that falls below $\theta_{\mathrm{lo}}$ while all slots are taken
waits and drains, up to $Q=500$ ticks in these runs, and then no longer
reaches a slot. Only the two smallest fleets, 4 robots on \emph{small} and 8
on \emph{narrow}, stay (almost) free of depletions, in 0 and 3 of their 240
runs with doubled drain. This is the
premise of \cref{prop:charge}, a bounded slot wait, failing; the
proposition gives a sufficient condition only, so it does not predict
where depletion starts; \cref{tab:reserve} in the appendix shows that the bounds measured even
with normal batteries would require a reserve 6.6 to 18.6 times the default.
Battery safety is therefore an empirical property of our default parameters,
not of the policy.

\noindent\textbf{Allocation coupling (Q2).}
\Cref{tab:main} is the controlled comparison. In none of the twelve
layout/fleet/load cells does the choice of cost model change throughput by
more than 2.9\% (warehouse, 32 robots, batch: 29.7 greedy vs.\ 30.6
Hungarian and Proxy vs.\ 30.3 \sname), and in every cell the four means lie
within one standard deviation of each other; the same holds for service
time. Paired by seed (\cref{tab:paired}), 35 of the 36 throughput intervals
against Hungarian contain zero; the exception is \sname\ on the console with
32 robots under batch load, $2.3\pm2.0\%$ \emph{below} Hungarian, fewer
exclusions than the 1.8 expected by chance among 36 intervals. Every interval
lies within $\pm3.7\%$ under the Poisson load and within $\pm7.1\%$ under
batch load, so the study excludes large effects of the cost model, not effects
of a few percent. This includes the cells where coupling could plausibly help: the
saturated batch regime with 32 robots, where 233--297 wait actions per run
show that robots do interact, and the light regime with 16--32 robots, where
robots are idle and the pairing decision is free to matter. Greedy
assignment is as good as optimal assignment throughout. The reason is
structural rather than statistical, and \cref{sec:disc} discusses it:
the quantity the coupling improves, the empty trip to the pickup, is a small
and well-predicted part of a task, and the congestion that exists arises at
endpoints rather than along paths.

\noindent\textbf{Planner backend (Q3, part 1).}
\Cref{tab:planner} and \cref{fig:planner} compare PP and windowed CBS on
\emph{small}, where CBS is affordable. CBS shortens the makespan by 2.8\%,
8.3\% and 8.3\% for 4, 6 and 8 robots (seed-paired: $-2.8\pm2.1$,
$-8.2\pm3.6$ and $-8.2\pm4.3\%$), cuts wait actions by 19--31\% and
service time by 6--10\%, at 0.09--0.34 planner ms per tick; it fell back
to PP for a group in 0.6 epochs per run, only at 6 robots. Since CBS and PP plan the same windows, the
gain is the cost of fixed priorities inside the window. The stress sweep also runs CBS on the larger layouts,
which the first round did not. With normal batteries it falls back to PP in
0.3 to 10 epochs per run (1.8 of about 148 on the warehouse with 32 robots,
10 of about 180 on \emph{narrow} with 16; over the whole sweep 8{,}240 of
its 8{,}242 fallbacks are node-budget exhaustions, the other two failed
searches). These counts exclude the robots that CBS defers to PP by
construction because they share a goal, such as up to
$\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}$ robots bound for one dock, so the backend is CBS for
robots with distinct goals followed by PP for the rest, not CBS alone. It
shortens the makespan by only 1.2--2.6\% (seed-paired over 120 pairs, e.g.\ $-1.9\pm0.6\%$ on the warehouse with 32 robots), against
4--12\% on \emph{small} in the same sweep, at 3 to 15 times the planner
time of PP.

\noindent\textbf{Scaling (Q3, part 2).}
\Cref{fig:scaling} (data in \cref{tab:scaling}) sweeps the warehouse fleet
from 4 to 48 robots under batch load. Throughput rises from 4.9 to 33.7 tasks
per 100 ticks and flattens beyond 40 robots, where utilisation has fallen to
0.41 because $9$ docks $\times\,\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}=3$ bound the number of
tasks in flight; service time falls from 1909 to 252 ticks. Planner cost
grows roughly linearly, from 0.02 to 0.75 ms per simulated tick for Hungarian
and to 0.97 for \sname; the path-aware pricing costs 35\% more A* calls at
48 robots (16{,}229 vs.\ 12{,}021 per run) and buys nothing measurable.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}\label{subsec:ablation}
\input{tables/ablation}

\Cref{tab:ablation} varies one setting at a time on the warehouse with 32
robots under batch load.

\noindent\textbf{Endpoint capacity is the dominant lever.}
Lowering the per-dock in-flight capacity from 3 to 2 drops throughput from
30.3 to 24.0 (\sname) and from 30.6 to 23.7 (Hungarian, \cref{tab:main}),
i.e.\ capacity 3 is worth $+26$--$29\%$ (seed-paired, capacity 2 costs
$20.7\pm3.3\%$ and $22.5\pm2.2\%$), and utilisation falls from 0.64 to
0.42 because tasks wait in the pool for a free dock. During development,
capacity 4 produced dock-front gridlocks in 2 of 30 runs under an earlier
priority scheme. With the final priorities, capacity 4 completes all 30 runs
of \cref{tab:cascade} (throughput 33.3, seed-paired $+9.0\pm1.4\%$ over capacity 3) but needs more than twice the holds (8.8 per run against 3.5), and
with static priorities it stalls in every one of them. We keep capacity 3 as
the default, which the first-round results were run with.

\noindent\textbf{Window and period trade throughput for planner time.}
A window of 10 ticks raises throughput to 32.4 ($+7\%$, seed-paired
$+6.9\pm1.6\%$) and halves wait actions (150 vs.\ 274) while cutting
planner time from 0.61 to 0.19 ms/tick; a window of 30 lowers throughput to
28.2 ($-6.9\pm3.9\%$) at 1.96 ms/tick. Longer
windows commit robots to longer reserved waits behind others and make
fall-backs more likely; \citet{li2021rhcr} likewise observe that RHCR
sometimes achieves higher throughput with shorter windows, because resolving
distant conflicts adds unnecessary waits. Replanning every tick ($h=1$) leaves throughput unchanged (30.3) at
$2.2\times$ the planner time. Disabling event-triggered epochs, so that idle
robots wait for the next periodic epoch, costs 1--5\% throughput and adds
55--125 wait actions; only the Hungarian loss is resolved by the paired
interval ($-5.2\pm3.3\%$, against $-1.2\pm5.2\%$ for \sname).

\noindent\textbf{Battery term and candidate pruning are neutral.}
Removing the battery factor ($w_{\mathrm{b}}=0$) changes throughput by less
than 0.5 for both cost models ($+0.01$ for \sname, $-0.43$ for Hungarian),
also in the battery stress (\cref{tab:battery}); with $\theta_{\mathrm{lo}}=0.2$ and the feasibility
check of \cref{eq:feasible}, charging decisions are rarely marginal. Pricing
3 or 12 candidates instead of 6 changes throughput by at most 0.2.

\subsection{Hold Cascade and Priorities}\label{subsec:cascade}
\input{tables/cascade}

\Cref{prop:pp} rests on the hold cascade, and the development history of
\cref{sec:disc} credits the dynamic priorities with removing a gridlock.
\Cref{tab:cascade} measures both on 30 seeds in four settings, including the
capacity-4 docks and the one-lane aisles. With the cascade, no run has an
executed conflict or stalls. Without it (\emph{no repair}: the failing robot
holds, but robots whose reservations cross its cell keep their paths),
conflicts are executed in 27, 30, 10 and 27 of the 30 runs per setting.
Re-planning the group with the failing robot promoted to the front, up to
three times, before holding without repair is no substitute: it avoids
conflicts on \emph{small} but not on the larger layouts (24, 30 and 16 of 30
runs). Neither alternative buys throughput: all three fallbacks deliver
within 0.7 tasks per 100 ticks of each other in every setting, so the
cascade's re-plans cost nothing measurable. Static priorities (status rank,
then id) keep every step conflict-free, because the cascade is still on,
but the fleet gridlocks: 30 of 30 runs stall on the warehouse, with and
without capacity-4 docks, and on \emph{narrow}, and 23 of 30 on
\emph{small}, with thousands of holds per run. Planning held and blocking
robots first is what keeps the loop live.

\subsection{Token-Passing Baselines}\label{subsec:baselines}
\input{tables/baselines}

TP \citep{ma2017lifelong} lets a robot that has reached the end of its path
take the pending task with the nearest pickup among those whose pickup and
dock are not the end of another robot's path, and plan its whole trip
(empty leg, loading dwell, loaded leg, final rest at the dock) against the
complete paths of all other robots, which all end in a permanent rest.
TPTS additionally lets the robot take over a task whose assignee has not yet
reached the pickup, if it gets there earlier; the displaced robot then
looks for a task itself. Our implementation keeps the paper's rules and adds
what our setting needs: the pickup dwell, the feasibility test
\cref{eq:feasible} with free-space distances, charger slots as endpoints, and
a return to the home bay for robots without a task (\cref{app:repro}).
Unlike Ma et al.'s instances, our pickups lie in the aisles, so our layouts
are not well-formed for TP and its completeness argument does not apply; in
the 300 TP and TPTS runs of \cref{tab:baselines} no step conflicts, no run
stalls and no robot depletes. Two of our additions could handicap the
baselines, and neither is active in these runs: no search hits the
20{,}000-expansion bound, and no earlier-arriving swap is dropped for want
of a path home for the displaced robot.

TP delivers 29--66\% less throughput than the rolling-horizon loop with
Hungarian costs, and TPTS 16--55\% less, in every setting. The gap is the
endpoint rule:
a task whose dock is the end of another robot's path is not eligible, which
limits every dock to one robot in flight. Run with the same limit
($\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}=1$), the rolling-horizon loop with Hungarian costs
and TPTS differ by $-2.5$ to $+1.6\%$ (TPTS relative to it, seed-paired) in
every setting, while TP stays 11--26\% behind it, a gap that task swaps
close. The baseline gap therefore measures dock capacity, not our allocation
or planner; and it is an observation about these adapted instances, not
about TP and TPTS on the well-formed instances they were designed for. TPTS pays for its swaps with 23{,}000--93{,}000 A*
calls per run, against 4{,}600--9{,}200 for the rolling-horizon loop.
\sname's path-aware costs stay neutral against Hungarian: at the default
capacity all five intervals contain zero, and at $\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}=1$
one of five excludes it by a hair ($+0.9\pm0.9\%$ under Poisson load).

\subsection{Coupling Regimes}\label{subsec:coupling}
\input{tables/coupling}

\Cref{sec:disc} argues that the coupling does not pay because the empty trip
is short and well predicted by the free-space distance and because
congestion concentrates at the docks. We defined four regimes that attack
those reasons, committed before their results (\cref{subsec:setup}): one-lane aisles, where a robot loading at
a pickup blocks the aisle and arrival times depend on traffic; hotspot
demand, which concentrates traffic in a few aisles; long empty trips from
the docks to far pickups under a light Poisson load, where robots are idle
and the choice of robot is free; and saturated chargers, where many robots
compete for 8 slots and the battery term of \cref{eq:cost} is active.
\Cref{tab:coupling} compares the four cost models on 30 paired seeds.

In the first three regimes the mean differences between \sname\ and
Hungarian or greedy assignment are at most 1.3\% in throughput and 0.6\% in
service time, and none of \sname's twelve intervals excludes zero; the
tightest ones, under the long empty trips, rule out effects above 0.5\%.
The only intervals in these regimes that exclude zero belong to the
congestion proxy and point both ways ($+1.7\pm1.4\%$ throughput on one-lane
aisles, $-1.5\pm1.3\%$ throughput and $+0.6\pm0.5\%$ service time under
hotspot demand, $-0.5\pm0.3\%$ service time on long trips, and
$-0.5\pm0.4\%$ against greedy there): small effects of either sign, not a
consistent gain. The
fourth regime is outside the envelope in which the fleet is safe: with
triple drain every method strands robots (586--641 depletion events over
30 runs) and 10--16 of its 30 runs stall. There \sname\ and the proxy show
$+23\pm18\%$ and $+24\pm21\%$ throughput against Hungarian, but service
time does not move ($0.0\pm2.2\%$) and throughput, being deliveries over the
time to the last delivery or the stall, mostly reflects fewer stalled runs
(10 for \sname, 16 for Hungarian). We do not count this as evidence for the
coupling. The negative result of \cref{subsec:main} therefore extends to the
regimes we chose to favour it.
