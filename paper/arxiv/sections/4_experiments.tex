\section{Experiments}\label{sec:exp}

We ask three questions. (Q1) Is the stack correct and stable in lifelong
operation---no conflicts, no depletions, no deadlocks---across layouts, fleet
sizes and load regimes? (Q2) Does pricing robot-to-pickup assignments with
reservation-aware arrival times (or a congestion proxy) improve throughput or
service time over static costs?
(Q3) Which of the remaining design choices (planner backend, window,
replanning period, endpoint capacity, battery term) matter, and what do they
cost in planner time? A second round of experiments, run after an adversarial
review of the first, adds a 60-seed stress sweep for Q1, an ablation of the
hold cascade and of the planning priorities, token-passing baselines, and
four regimes in which we expected the coupling to matter most for Q2
(\cref{subsec:cascade,subsec:baselines,subsec:coupling}). A third round, run
after an external deep review and pre-registered in the repository before its
results existed, tests Q2 without event-triggered epochs, varies $K$ in cells
where the pool exceeds it and splits the static-priority arm
(\cref{subsec:nondeg,subsec:ablation,subsec:cascade}).

\subsection{Experimental Setup}\label{subsec:setup}

\noindent\textbf{Layouts.}
\emph{warehouse} ($52\times19$, 715 free cells) has four blocks of five
10-cell shelf lines with 2-wide aisles, 100 pickup points, 9 docks, 4 charger
stations of 2 slots and 48 home bays. \emph{console} ($36\times25$, 658 free
cells) is generated from the zone/line/dock counts that appear in the
operator console's wireframe labels (zones A--D with 3, 3, 2 and 4 shelf
lines; two named shipping docks, modelled as bays of three delivery cells), 84
pickups, 6 docks, 3 stations, 32 bays. \emph{small} ($16\times10$, 104 free
cells) is an open floor with 4 pickups, 2 docks, 2 slots and 8 bays used for
the planner comparison. \emph{narrow} ($52\times15$, 496 free cells) repeats
the warehouse pattern with one-lane aisles: four blocks of four shelf lines
whose single aisle row is closed by shelves on both sides, so robots cannot
pass inside an aisle and a robot loading at a pickup blocks it; 80 pickups, 6
docks, 8 slots, 32 bays. \Cref{tab:layouts} in the appendix lists all counts.

\noindent\textbf{Tasks and load.}
Each run delivers 200 tasks (100 on \emph{small}, and 100 on every layout
in the stress sweep) whose pickup and dock are
sampled uniformly. Two load regimes are used: a Poisson stream of
$\lambda=0.15$ tasks per tick, which is below the capacity of fleets of 16 or
more robots and saturates a fleet of 8, and \emph{batch}, which releases all
tasks at $t=0$ and measures pure throughput. The stress sweep uses
$\lambda=0.3$ on \emph{small}, and the long-trips regime of
\cref{subsec:coupling} $\lambda=0.1$. Loading and unloading dwell is
$\delta=2$ ticks. Initial charge is uniform in $[0.5,1.0]$, except in the
battery stress where it is uniform in $[0.2,0.4]$ so that every robot has to
charge during the run. The stress sweep adds a harder battery regime, initial
charge in $[0.2,0.4]$ with doubled drain ($\beta_{\mathrm{m}}=0.002$,
$\beta_{\mathrm{w}}=0.0004$). The coupling regimes use two non-uniform demand
models: \emph{hotspot}, where 80\% of pickups are drawn from the 10\% of
pickup points nearest the floor centre, and \emph{far}, where pickups are
drawn only from the 25\% of pickup points farthest from any dock. All
parameters are listed in \cref{tab:params}.

\noindent\textbf{Methods.}
Four allocation cost models share the same planner, charging thresholds and
endpoint capacities: \emph{Greedy} (cheapest pair first, static distance),
\emph{Hungarian} (optimal assignment, static distance), \emph{Proxy}
(Hungarian on distance plus reservation-table congestion, \cref{eq:cost}) and
\emph{\sname} (Hungarian on windowed A* arrival times). The charging policy is
the same for all four, except that the greedy baseline also matches low-charge
robots to charger slots greedily. The planner backend
is prioritized planning (PP) with the hold cascade unless stated; windowed
CBS is compared on \emph{small} and, in the stress sweep, on every layout.
Defaults are $w=20$, $h=5$, $K=6$, $\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}=3$,
$\kappa_{\mathrm{p}}=1$, $w_{\mathrm{b}}=1$. As baselines we re-implement
token passing (TP) and token passing with task swaps (TPTS)
\citep{ma2017lifelong} in the same simulator, with the same battery model,
dwell, charging thresholds and feasibility test (\cref{subsec:baselines}).

\noindent\textbf{Metrics.}
Throughput is delivered tasks per 100 ticks of makespan, the makespan being
the tick of the last delivery or, for a run that stalls or reaches the tick
limit, the tick at which it stops; service time is delivery minus arrival,
averaged over the delivered tasks; wait time is first assignment minus arrival;
utilisation is the share of robot-ticks in which a robot holds an assigned,
undelivered task; wait actions count
ticks a robot with a goal did not move; holds count planner fallbacks; planner
ms/tick is wall-clock time of allocation plus planning divided by simulated
ticks; conflicts are counted on the executed step (vertex and swap) from the
robots' cells, independently of the reservation table, both by the simulator
and by the trajectory auditor (\cref{subsec:planning}), which also reports
illegal moves, the largest trip delay $W$ and the longest slot wait $Q$ of
\cref{prop:charge}; a run is flagged \emph{stalled} (``deadlocked'' in the
code) when no task is picked up or delivered for 1000 consecutive ticks
while tasks are outstanding, and a run that neither finishes nor stalls stops
at 20{,}000 ticks with its unfinished tasks counted. The first-round sweeps
use seeds 1--5, the stress sweep seeds 1--60 and the other
sweeps seeds 1--30; results are reported as mean\stdv{std}. Runs with the
same seed see the same task stream and initial charge, so we compare
variants by seed-paired relative differences with two-sided 95\%
Student-$t$ intervals ($n-1$ degrees of freedom; \cref{tab:paired} in the
appendix for the first round). The intervals are reported comparison by
comparison, without a correction for multiple comparisons, so among many
intervals a few are expected to exclude zero by chance; we say so where we
count exclusions. For the two families in which we read individual intervals
as effects, the fourteen lever and planner comparisons of \cref{tab:paired}
and the eight fallback comparisons of \cref{subsec:cascade}, we also apply
Holm's step-down procedure at a family-wise level of 5\% to the paired
$t$-tests and say which differences survive it; we call the others
\emph{nominal}.

\noindent\textbf{Hardware and protocol.}
All runs are single-threaded Node.js~22 on an AMD Ryzen~5 5500GT. Runs are
deterministic given the seed: the only random draws are the task stream and
the initial charge, from one seeded generator. All 8{,}340 runs of this
paper were produced at one commit of the repository (\texttt{acbfe97}), on 11
worker processes; no simulated quantity depends on the number of workers
(\cref{app:repro}). The records also hold wall-clock fields, which depend on
the host and its load, and we do not report those. The planner times in this
paper were measured separately, on the same host while it was otherwise
idle: the planner, scaling and ablation sweeps were run in one process, a
repeat was started only at a one-minute load average of at most 0.5, and a
repeat counted only if that load average was at most 1.7 right after it.
We made four attempts and used the three valid ones (the first was
invalid, with a load average of 1.73 after it, and is kept in the
repository). For every configuration we report the median over the three
valid repeats of the mean over seeds, with the range in the tables. The
largest spread between the valid repeats is 6\% of the smallest value.
The timing was run at commit \texttt{6b64f93}; the code under
\texttt{src/fms}, the benchmark driver and the record schema are identical at
that commit and at the commit of the results (\texttt{git diff} between them
is empty). We call the main, planner, scaling, ablation and battery
sweeps the first round (420 runs), the stress, cascade, baseline, coupling and
token-passing sweeps the second round, and the three sweeps of
\cref{subsec:nondeg,subsec:ablation,subsec:cascade} the third round
(1{,}140 runs). The third round is pre-registered in
\path{paper/experiments/PREREG_v2.md}, which commit \texttt{f35437f} adds
alone, before any record of these sweeps exists in the history; the
document states that the regime without event epochs was chosen after a
hint. As for the second round, the history shows the order of commits only. The
stress, cascade, baseline and coupling sweeps, including the coupling
regimes, are defined in commit \texttt{4a72ff1} of the repository, which
precedes the first commit of their results (\texttt{dccf403}) by 36 minutes
on the same day, by committer timestamp; later commits changed only what the
auditor and the simulator record, not the sweeps or the simulated behaviour.
The history shows the order of commits, not that no run preceded the first
one, and a gap of 36 minutes is weak evidence of a choice made in advance.
The sweep of token-passing variants (\cref{tab:tpfree}) was added later,
when the baseline results were known. The benchmark
driver, the per-run JSON records and the summary CSVs are committed with the
code.

\subsection{Main Results}\label{subsec:main}
\input{tables/main}

\noindent\textbf{Correctness and stability (Q1).}
Over the 420 first-round runs---8{,}833{,}050 robot-steps, 5{,}317{,}603
move actions and 81{,}000 delivered tasks---the executed steps contain zero
vertex or swap conflicts, as \cref{cor:exec} requires, according to both the
simulator and the independent auditor, no robot ever depletes, and every run
delivers all of its tasks without tripping the stall detector. These runs
cover three layouts, fleets of 4 to 48 robots, both load regimes, the four
allocation methods and both planner backends, but not every combination of
them (\cref{subsec:setup}). Hold fallbacks are rare (at most 5.2 per run on average in the
main sweep, reached with 32 robots under batch load). The battery stress
(\cref{tab:battery}) forces 22.6--23.2 charging sessions per run for 16
robots and still records zero depletions, with a final mean charge of
0.34--0.38.

\input{tables/stress}
The stress sweep (\cref{tab:stress}) repeats this check on 60 seeds for every
layout including \emph{narrow}, two or three fleet sizes per layout, both
planner backends, both load regimes and both battery regimes: 4{,}320 runs
and 51.6 million robot-steps. The auditor finds no vertex conflict, no swap
conflict and no illegal move in any of them. With normal batteries (2{,}160
runs) no robot depletes, no run stalls and every task is delivered; the
longest interval without a pickup or delivery is 126 ticks and the lowest
charge any robot reaches is 0.044. Under doubled drain the picture changes:
robots deplete in 1{,}476 of 2{,}160 runs (9{,}535 depletion events), and
227 runs stall with 510 tasks undelivered, every one of them after a
depletion, because a stranded robot is a permanent obstacle. The depletions
are not planner failures but the charging policy running out of slots: a
robot that falls below $\theta_{\mathrm{lo}}$ while all slots are taken
waits and drains, up to $Q=500$ ticks in these runs, and then no longer
reaches a slot. Only the two smallest fleets, 4 robots on \emph{small} and 8
on \emph{narrow}, stay (almost) free of depletions, in 0 and 3 of their 240
runs with doubled drain. This is the
premise of \cref{prop:charge}, a bounded slot wait, failing; the
proposition gives a sufficient condition only, so it does not predict
where depletion starts; \cref{tab:reserve} in the appendix shows that the bounds measured even
with normal batteries would require a reserve 6.6 to 18.6 times the default.
Battery safety is therefore an empirical property of our default parameters,
not of the policy.

\noindent\textbf{Allocation coupling (Q2).}
\Cref{tab:main} is the controlled comparison. In none of the twelve
layout/fleet/load cells does the choice of cost model change throughput by
more than 2.9\% (warehouse, 32 robots, batch: 29.7 greedy vs.\ 30.6
Hungarian and Proxy vs.\ 30.3 \sname), and in every cell the four throughput
means differ by less than the smallest of their four standard deviations;
the service-time means differ by less than the largest of theirs. Paired by seed (\cref{tab:paired}), 35 of the 36 throughput intervals
against Hungarian contain zero; the exception is \sname\ on the console with
32 robots under batch load, $2.3\pm2.0\%$ \emph{below} Hungarian. One
exclusion is no more than chance would produce: 36 independent 95\%
intervals of true-zero effects would give 1.8 on average, and ours share
baselines and seeds, so they are not independent. Every interval
lies within $\pm3.7\%$ under the Poisson load and within $\pm7.1\%$ under
batch load. Four of the twelve cells cannot show a throughput effect at all:
under the Poisson load a fleet of 16 or 32 robots keeps up with the stream,
so the makespan is set by the last arrival (on the same seed it differs by
at most 4.3\% between 16 and 32 robots) and throughput, which is tasks over
makespan, with it. The twelve throughput intervals of those cells are
therefore uninformative, and service time is the metric to read there.
Paired by seed, all 36 service-time intervals against Hungarian contain zero
(smallest $p=0.07$); their bounds reach $10.8\%$ (half-widths up to $\pm6.6\%$). Read cell by cell, the intervals exclude effects larger than
these bounds; they do not show that an effect is absent. The study was not
designed or powered as an equivalence test and fixes no equivalence margin
in advance, so we claim only that no large effect of the cost model was
detected, and effects of a few percent remain unresolved. The cells include
the saturated batch regime with 32 robots, where 233--297 wait actions per run
show that robots do interact. Greedy assignment is not measurably worse than
optimal assignment in any cell. That statement is weak, because in most calls
greedy and Hungarian assignment return the same matching
(\cref{tab:diagnostic}); \cref{subsec:nondeg} repeats the comparison in a regime
where they disagree more often.

\input{tables/diagnostic}
\Cref{tab:diagnostic} shows how little the coupled cost changes in these
cells. The simulator records, next to every allocation, how the estimate of
each priced robot--task pair differs from the free-space distance, whether
the matching differs from the Hungarian matching on the static costs of the
same robots and tasks, and how many robots and tasks the allocation call has.
Three causes keep the cost mostly inert, and the window is only one of them.
\emph{The window.} For 60--86\% of the priced pairs the pickup lies
beyond the window, where the estimate ignores reservations after the first
$w$ ticks (\cref{eq:arrival}). The path-aware estimate differs from the
free-space distance for 6--26\% of the pairs, by 1.9--3.0 ticks on average
against a mean distance of 23--40, and it changes the matching in 1--14\% of
the allocations. The congestion proxy differs for 20--58\% of the pairs and
changes the matching in 5--30\% of the allocations, with the same outcome.
\emph{The dock gate.} Only tasks whose dock and pickup have spare
capacity enter the candidate pool. With 32 robots the pool holds a single task
in 74--87\% of the calls, and with 8 robots a single robot is eligible in
82--90\%. \emph{Event-triggered epochs.} An epoch starts whenever a robot
becomes idle while tasks are pending, which offers the allocation one robot,
and through the dock gate one newly eligible task, at a time.
\Cref{subsec:nondeg} measures this one: in the two 32-robot cells it moves the
share of calls with at least two robots and two tasks from 14--17\% to 56--59\%.
Taken together, only 9--26\% of the calls in the twelve cells pose a real
assignment problem, and in 91--99\% greedy and Hungarian assignment return the
same matching on \sname's cost matrix. The cost is not idle in the other calls:
in the warehouse with 32 robots under batch load, 63 of its 95 matching
changes fall in calls with a single robot or a single task, where it decides
which robot takes that task. The null result is therefore a statement about
allocation under a binding dock gate with event-triggered epochs, made with a
cost that seldom has an assignment problem to solve: changing the matching in at
most one allocation in seven did not move throughput or service time
measurably. It does not show that congestion-aware assignment cannot help.
Beyond the window the estimate is a free-space distance. The number of priced
candidates $K$ binds in some cells and not in others, and varying it
changed nothing measurable where it binds (\cref{subsec:ablation}).
\Cref{sec:disc} adds what these counters do not measure: the empty trip to
the pickup is a small part of a task, and the congestion that exists arises
at endpoints rather than along paths.

\noindent\textbf{Planner backend (Q3, part 1).}
\Cref{tab:planner} and \cref{fig:planner} compare PP and windowed CBS on
\emph{small}, where CBS is affordable. CBS shortens the makespan by 2.8\%,
8.3\% and 8.3\% for 4, 6 and 8 robots (seed-paired: $-2.8\pm2.1$,
$-8.2\pm3.6$ and $-8.2\pm4.3\%$), cuts wait actions by 19--31\% and
service time by 6--10\%, while expanding 1.03, 1.32 and 1.07 times as many
A* nodes per tick as PP (0.09--0.30 planner ms per tick); it fell back
to PP for a group in 0.6 epochs per run, only at 6 robots. With five seeds,
of these three makespan differences only the one at 6 robots survives the
Holm correction; the stress sweep below repeats the comparison on 120 seed
pairs per fleet size. Since CBS and PP plan the same windows, the
gain is the cost of fixed priorities inside the window. The stress sweep also runs CBS on the larger layouts,
which the first round did not. With normal batteries it falls back to PP in
0.3 to 10 epochs per run (1.8 of about 148 on the warehouse with 32 robots,
10 of about 180 on \emph{narrow} with 16; over the whole sweep 8{,}240 of
its 8{,}242 fallbacks are node-budget exhaustions, the other two failed
searches). These counts exclude the robots that CBS defers to PP by
construction because they share a goal, such as up to
$\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}$ robots bound for one dock, so the backend is CBS for
robots with distinct goals followed by PP for the rest, not CBS alone. On
\emph{small} it shortens the makespan by 4--12\% (seed-paired over 120
pairs: $-3.9\pm0.6$, $-8.7\pm0.7$ and $-11.6\pm0.9\%$ for 4, 6 and 8
robots). Split by load, with 60 pairs each, the gains are $-3.8\pm0.7$,
$-9.1\pm1.1$ and $-12.6\pm1.4\%$ under batch load and $-4.1\pm0.9$,
$-8.4\pm0.9$ and $-10.5\pm1.3\%$ under the Poisson load; both loads saturate
\emph{small}, so the pooled intervals are not misleading. On the larger layouts the gain under batch load is 2.4--4.1\%
(60 pairs, e.g.\ $-3.8\pm1.0\%$ on the warehouse with 32 robots); under the
Poisson load the makespan is mostly set by the last arrival and the
difference is at most 2.5\%. For that gain CBS expands 2.1 to 12.2 times as
many A* nodes per tick as PP on the larger layouts under batch load.

\noindent\textbf{Scaling (Q3, part 2).}
\Cref{fig:scaling} (data in \cref{tab:scaling}) sweeps the warehouse fleet
from 4 to 48 robots under batch load. Throughput rises from 4.9 to 33.7 tasks
per 100 ticks and flattens beyond 40 robots, where utilisation has fallen to
0.41 because $9$ docks $\times\,\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}=3$ bound the number of
tasks in flight; service time falls from 1909 to 252 ticks. Planner cost
grows faster than the fleet and then flattens with the throughput: with
Hungarian costs the A* expansions per tick grow 56-fold for 12 times as many
robots, from 18 at 4 robots to 752 at 32 and 1{,}021 at 48 (planner time from
0.02 to 0.77 ms per simulated tick, and to 0.98 for \sname); the path-aware
pricing costs 35\% more A* calls at
48 robots (16{,}229 vs.\ 12{,}021 per run) and 25\% more expansions per
tick, and buys nothing measurable.

\noindent\textbf{Epoch latency.}
The means above hide the time of a single epoch, which is what a real-time
deadline would have to bound. \Cref{tab:epoch} gives the wall time of one
rolling-horizon epoch on the quiet host, from every epoch of every seed. For

\subsection{Ablation Studies}\label{subsec:ablation}
\input{tables/ablation}

\Cref{tab:ablation} varies one setting at a time on the warehouse with 32
robots under batch load.

\noindent\textbf{Endpoint capacity is the dominant lever.}
Lowering the per-dock in-flight capacity from 3 to 2 drops throughput from
30.3 to 24.0 (\sname) and from 30.6 to 23.7 (Hungarian, \cref{tab:main}),
i.e.\ capacity 3 is worth $+26$--$29\%$ (seed-paired, capacity 2 costs
$20.7\pm3.3\%$ and $22.5\pm2.2\%$), and utilisation falls from 0.64 to
0.42 because tasks wait in the pool for a free dock. During development,
capacity 4 produced dock-front gridlocks in some runs under an earlier
priority scheme; those runs are not part of the committed benchmark. With the final priorities, capacity 4 completes all 30 runs
of \cref{tab:cascade} (throughput 33.3, seed-paired $+9.0\pm1.4\%$ over capacity 3) but needs more than twice the holds (8.8 per run against 3.5), and
with static priorities it stalls in every one of them. We keep capacity 3 as
the default, which the first-round results were run with.

\noindent\textbf{Window and period trade throughput for planner time.}
A window of 10 ticks raises throughput to 32.4 ($+7\%$, seed-paired
$+6.9\pm1.6\%$) and halves wait actions (150 vs.\ 274) while cutting
the A* expansions per tick to 0.21 of the default
(planner time from 0.62 to 0.19 ms/tick); a window of 30 lowers throughput to
28.2 ($-6.9\pm3.9\%$, nominal) at 3.5 times the expansions per tick (2.00 ms/tick). Longer
windows commit robots to longer reserved waits behind others and make
fallbacks more likely; \citet{li2021rhcr} likewise observe that RHCR
sometimes achieves higher throughput with shorter windows, because resolving
distant conflicts adds unnecessary waits, although in most of their cases a
finite window changes throughput by less than 1\%. Replanning every tick ($h=1$) shows no throughput change (30.3;
seed-paired $-0.1\pm4.9\%$, an interval too wide to call it none) at
$2.2\times$ the A* expansions per tick. Disabling event-triggered epochs, so that idle
robots wait for the next periodic epoch, costs 1--5\% throughput and adds
55--125 wait actions; only the Hungarian loss is resolved, and only at the
nominal level ($-5.2\pm3.3\%$, against $-1.2\pm5.2\%$ for \sname). Of the
fourteen lever and planner intervals of \cref{tab:paired}, four survive the
Holm correction: dock capacity for both cost models, the window of 10 and
CBS at 6 robots.

\noindent\textbf{The battery term shows no resolved effect.}
Removing the battery factor ($w_{\mathrm{b}}=0$) changes throughput by less
than 0.5 for both cost models ($+0.01$ for \sname, $-0.43$ for Hungarian),
also in the battery stress (\cref{tab:battery}); with $\theta_{\mathrm{lo}}=0.2$ and the feasibility
check of \cref{eq:feasible}, charging decisions are rarely marginal. With
five seeds these two comparisons have seed-paired intervals of
$\pm4.0$ to $\pm4.5\%$, so they exclude large effects only.

\input{tables/kabl}
\noindent\textbf{The candidate count $K$.}
Pricing 3 or 12 candidates instead of 6 changes throughput by at most 0.13 on
the warehouse with 32 robots, but that cell cannot exercise $K$: its pool holds a
single task in 81\% of the calls, and one task is the same candidate for every $K$.
So $K=6$ cannot be read as a bound on what the cost sees from that cell. We therefore
repeated the ablation, pre-registered, in two cells where the
pool is larger, the warehouse with 8 robots and \emph{narrow} with 16, on 30 seeds
(\cref{tab:kabl}). There $K$ does bind: the cost prices 3.4, 6.6 and 8.9 pairs per
allocation call at $K=3$, 6 and 12 in the first cell and 4.1, 6.0 and 7.1 in the
second, and the run of every seed differs from its run at $K=6$. Throughput does
not follow: the means differ from $K=6$ by at most 0.8\%, the intervals are
$\pm0.4$ to $\pm1.3\%$ wide, and Holm's procedure over the four comparisons
rejects none. So we found no effect of $K$ in these cells, with those bounds, and
we do not list $K$ among the choices that limit the cost.

\subsection{Hold Cascade and Priorities}\label{subsec:cascade}
\input{tables/cascade}

\Cref{prop:pp} rests on the hold cascade, and the development history of
\cref{sec:disc} credits the dynamic priorities with removing a gridlock.
\Cref{tab:cascade} measures both on 30 seeds in four settings, including the
capacity-4 docks and the one-lane aisles. With the cascade, no run has an
executed conflict or stalls. Without it (\emph{no repair}: the failing robot
holds, but robots whose reservations cross its cell keep their paths),
conflicts are executed in 27, 30, 10 and 27 of the 30 runs per setting.
Re-planning the group with the failing robot promoted to the front, up to
three times, before holding without repair is no substitute: it avoids
conflicts on \emph{small} but not on the larger layouts (24, 30 and 16 of 30
runs). The three fallbacks deliver
within 0.63 tasks per 100 ticks of each other in every setting, but the
throughput of variants that execute conflicts is not comparable with that of a
conflict-free variant, because robots pass through each other. With capacity-4
docks, \emph{no repair} executes 15.0 conflicts per run on average and up to 41,
and \emph{restarts} 15.2 and up to 39 (\cref{tab:cascade}). Paired by seed,
dropping the repair delivers $+2.0\pm1.2\%$ more with capacity-4 docks, the one
difference among the eight that survives the Holm correction; restarts deliver
$+1.7\pm1.3\%$ there and $+1.0\pm1.0\%$ on \emph{narrow} at the nominal level, and the other five
intervals contain zero. These differences do not measure a cost of the cascade's
re-plans: the reference variants execute conflicts, so their plans are not
feasible, and the differences show what executing conflicts is worth to
throughput. We have no conflict-free fallback that does not re-plan
(\cref{sec:disc}).

\input{tables/split}
Static priorities (status rank,
then id) keep every step conflict-free, because the cascade is still on,
but the fleet gridlocks: 30 of 30 runs stall on the warehouse, with and
without capacity-4 docks, and on \emph{narrow}, and 23 of 30 on
\emph{small}, with thousands of holds per run. That arm switches off three
things at once: the blocking-first ordering, the held-first ordering and the
admission of held parking robots to the first planning group.
\Cref{tab:split} separates them on the same settings and seeds, in a
pre-registered sweep; the arm \emph{held first only} keeps the held-first ordering
together with the admission of held parking robots, the arm \emph{blocking first only}
the blocking-first ordering. Held-first ordering alone keeps every
run live (no stalled run in 120) and stays 0.7--3.0\% below the dynamic ordering;
the interval excludes zero in three of the four settings. Blocking-first
ordering alone stalls 16 of 120 runs, 9 and 5 of them in the two warehouse
settings, and is up to 21.6\% below the dynamic ordering. Both single-mechanism arms are far
ahead of static priorities in every setting, and Holm's procedure over the eight
comparisons rejects all of them. The pre-registered decision rule, applied
mechanically, returns that the advantage needs the combination in the two
warehouse settings, that blocking-first ordering accounts for it on
\emph{small}, and that either suffices on \emph{narrow}. That rule asks for an
interval against the dynamic ordering that contains zero, which held-first
ordering alone misses by 2--3\% on the warehouse although it avoids every stall.
We therefore credit the dynamic ordering as a whole: held-first ordering is
what keeps the loop live, and the full ordering recovers the last few percent
of throughput.

\subsection{Token-Passing Baselines}\label{subsec:baselines}
\input{tables/baselines}

TP \citep{ma2017lifelong} lets a robot that has reached the end of its path
take the pending task with the nearest pickup among those whose pickup and
dock are not the end of another robot's path, and plan its whole trip
(empty leg, loading dwell, loaded leg, final rest at the dock) against the
complete paths of all other robots, which all end in a permanent rest.
TPTS additionally lets the robot take over a task whose assignee has not yet
reached the pickup, if it gets there earlier; the displaced robot then
looks for a task itself. Our implementation keeps the paper's rules and adds
what our setting needs: the pickup dwell, the feasibility test
\cref{eq:feasible} with free-space distances, charger slots as endpoints, and
a return to the home bay for robots without a task (\cref{app:repro}). Their
goal test at a pickup asks only that the pickup stay free for the loading dwell,
whereas the rolling-horizon loop needs it free until the horizon
(\cref{subsec:planning}); this can only delay the loop's own arrivals.
Unlike Ma et al.'s instances, our pickups lie in the aisles, so our layouts
are not well-formed for TP and its completeness argument does not apply; in
the 300 TP and TPTS runs of \cref{tab:baselines} no step conflicts, no run
stalls and no robot depletes. Two of our additions could handicap the
baselines and are not active in these runs: no search hits the
20{,}000-expansion bound, and no earlier-arriving swap is dropped for want
of a path home for the displaced robot. A third one is active: our robot
without a task drives to its own home bay and takes no task on the way,
whereas in Ma et al.\ a free agent rests where its path ended, moves only
off the delivery location of a pending task, and may, as an option they
describe, request the token at every step. \Cref{tab:tpfree} measures this difference on the same settings
and seeds with two more variants: the drive home made interruptible, and the
published rule, adapted (the robot rests where its path ended and requests the
token at every tick; it moves only off the delivery cell of a pending task, as
in Ma et al., or, as two added triggers, off the pickup cell of a pending task
or off a charger slot, to the nearest home bay or dock in which no other path
ends and to which no pending task delivers; pickups are not endpoints of this
move because they lie on the aisles). No run of either
variant has an executed conflict, stalls or depletes a robot.

TP delivers 29--66\% less throughput than the rolling-horizon loop with
Hungarian costs, and TPTS 16--55\% less, in every setting. The gap comes mainly
from the endpoint rule:
a task whose dock is the end of another robot's path is not eligible, which
limits every dock to one robot in flight. Run with the same limit
($\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}=1$), the rolling-horizon loop with Hungarian costs
and TPTS differ by $-2.5$ to $+1.6\%$ (TPTS relative to it, seed-paired) in
every setting, while TP stays 11--26\% behind it, a gap that task swaps
close. The gap to TPTS therefore measures dock capacity, not our allocation
or planner; and it is an observation about these adapted instances, not
about TP and TPTS on the well-formed instances they were designed for. TPTS pays for its swaps with 23{,}000--93{,}000 A*
calls per run, against 4{,}600--9{,}200 for the rolling-horizon loop at its
default dock capacity; calls are not equal units, and in node expansions TPTS
needs 0.79--3.21 million per run against 0.18--0.56 million for the loop, which
is 3.3--6.2 times the expansions of Hungarian assignment.

\input{tables/tpfree}
The rule for idle robots matters for TPTS and not for TP
(\cref{tab:tpfree}). TP changes by at most 1.0\% under either variant. With
an interruptible drive home, TPTS gains 1.3--3.7\% in every setting (all
five intervals exclude zero) and then delivers 0.9--3.7\% more than the
rolling-horizon loop at $\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}=1$, again with all five
intervals above zero. With the published rule, adapted, TPTS lies, across the five settings, between 9.7\%
below and 2.9\% above our home variant and between 10.7\% below and 2.9\%
above the loop at $\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}=1$: a robot that rests in a dock keeps
that dock out of reach of the endpoint rule until a task for it arrives. At
one robot in flight per dock, token passing with task swaps and an
interruptible drive home is thus ahead of our loop in every setting, so at equal
capacity our loop is slightly behind the best token-passing variant. Two of
the five intervals (warehouse with 32 robots, $p=0.033$, and narrow, $p=0.032$)
do not survive a Holm correction over the ten intervals of the home and
interruptible variants against the loop. Under every idle rule the gap at the default
capacity remains (TPTS at least 12\% behind).

Against Hungarian, \sname's path-aware costs show no resolved difference
except one marginal one: at the default capacity all five intervals contain
zero, and at $\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}=1$
one of five excludes it by a hair ($+0.9\pm0.9\%$ under Poisson load).

\subsection{Coupling Regimes}\label{subsec:coupling}
\input{tables/coupling}

\Cref{sec:disc} explains the null result by two conjectures: the empty trip
is short and well predicted by the free-space distance, and congestion
concentrates at the docks. We defined four regimes that attack
those reasons, defined before their results were committed (\cref{subsec:setup}): one-lane aisles, where a robot loading at
a pickup blocks the aisle and arrival times depend on traffic; hotspot
demand, which concentrates traffic in a few aisles; long empty trips from
the docks to far pickups under a light Poisson load, where robots are idle
and the choice of robot is free; and saturated chargers, where many robots
compete for 8 slots and the battery term of \cref{eq:cost} is active.
\Cref{tab:coupling} compares the four cost models on 30 paired seeds.
The counters of \cref{tab:diagnostic} show how far each regime exercises
the coupling. Under hotspot demand the path-aware cost changes the matching
in 27\% of the allocations, about twice the share of any cell of the main
study. In the other three it changes less: 6\% in the one-lane aisles, 3\%
under long empty trips, where 92\% of the priced pickups lie beyond the
window, and 2\% with saturated chargers. Only the hotspot regime is a
stronger test than the main study; the long-trips regime compares nearly
identical treatments.

In the first three regimes the mean differences between \sname\ and
Hungarian or greedy assignment are at most 1.3\% in throughput and 0.6\% in
service time, and none of \sname's twelve intervals excludes zero; the
tightest ones, under the long empty trips, exclude service-time effects above 0.5\%.
Throughput there is set by the last arrival: on the same seed the four methods finish
within 2 to 31 ticks of each other, so its intervals are tight by construction, and we
quote service time only. The two costs also rarely differ there.
The only intervals in these regimes that exclude zero belong to the
congestion proxy and point both ways ($+1.7\pm1.4\%$ throughput on one-lane
aisles, $-1.5\pm1.3\%$ throughput and $+0.6\pm0.5\%$ service time under
hotspot demand, $-0.5\pm0.3\%$ service time on long trips, and
$-0.5\pm0.4\%$ against greedy there): small effects of either sign, not a
consistent gain. The
fourth regime is outside the envelope in which the fleet is safe: with
triple drain every method strands robots (586--641 depletion events over
30 runs) and 10--16 of its 30 runs stall. There \sname\ and the proxy show
$+23\pm18\%$ and $+24\pm21\%$ throughput against Hungarian, but service
time does not move ($0.0\pm2.2\%$) and throughput, being deliveries over the
time to the last delivery or the stall, mostly reflects fewer stalled runs
(10 for \sname, 16 for Hungarian). A stalled run has delivered 193 (Hungarian) or 197
(\sname) of its 200 tasks on average when it stops, and the 1{,}001 ticks of the
stall detector are part of its makespan. Without that tail, mean throughput is
10.01 for Hungarian and 10.48 for \sname, a difference of $+4.7\%$, against $+15.0\%$
for the same means with the tail. Static greedy assignment, which also
matches robots to charger slots greedily, shows a comparable, unresolved $+20.4\pm22.4\%$ against Hungarian (11 stalled runs),
and against greedy assignment neither path-aware interval excludes zero
($+12.5\pm17.2\%$ for \sname, $+9.7\pm16.2\%$ for the proxy). We do not
count this as evidence for the coupling. Within the safe envelope, the null result of \cref{subsec:main}
therefore also holds in these regimes, of which only one changes the
matching often, with
the same caveat that it is not an equivalence test. These regimes still use
the pickup-leg cost and the dock-capacity gate; they do not test a
full-trip, reservation-aware score such as that of \citet{hirayama2026detour}
or fleets of the size used by \citet{zhang2026flow} and
\citet{gaber2026grand}.

\subsection{A Regime Without Event-Triggered Epochs}\label{subsec:nondeg}
\input{tables/nondeg}

The counters of \cref{tab:diagnostic} say that most allocation calls of the main
study are degenerate, and the ablation of \cref{subsec:ablation} gave a hint:
without event-triggered epochs, \sname\ was above Hungarian by
$+3.3\pm3.0\%$ in throughput (five seeds, nominal, not corrected for the
fourteen comparisons of \cref{tab:paired}). An event-triggered epoch starts
whenever a robot becomes idle while tasks are pending, which hands the
allocation one idle robot at a time. We tested the hint on 30 seeds in the
warehouse and the console layout with 32 robots under batch load, for all four
cost models, with a reference arm that keeps event epochs on and an optional
arm with $\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}=4$ (\cref{tab:nondeg}). \textbf{We chose this regime after seeing the hint, so the test is
confirmatory of a post-hoc observation, not blind.} Hypotheses, configurations, tests and decision rules
were committed in \path{paper/experiments/PREREG_v2.md} (commit
\texttt{f35437f}) before any of these runs existed, and the first five seeds of the warehouse
cell reproduce the earlier ablation records exactly.

Without event epochs the allocation is a real assignment problem much more often:
56--59\% of the calls have at least two robots and two tasks, against 14--17\% with
event epochs, and \sname\ changes the matching in 21\% and 22\% of the calls
in the two cells. The primary comparison, \sname\ against Hungarian in
throughput, gives $+0.4\pm1.1\%$ on the warehouse ($p=0.43$) and $+0.6\pm1.5\%$ on
the console ($p=0.42$). Holm's procedure over these two rejects neither, and the
hint does not replicate. The reference arm with event epochs gives $+1.1\pm1.1\%$
($p=0.053$) and $+0.1\pm1.5\%$. The difference between the effects without and with event epochs is $-0.7\pm1.5\%$ and
$+0.5\pm1.6\%$, so we cannot tell that the switch changes the effect of the
coupling. Greedy and Hungarian assignment agree in 74--90\% of the calls here,
and greedy differs from Hungarian by $0.0\pm0.8\%$ and $+0.2\pm1.0\%$:
greedy assignment is not measurably worse than optimal assignment also in a regime where
a quarter of the calls or more are decided differently. With the looser dock gate ($\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}=4$)
\sname\ is $+1.5\pm1.5\%$ and $+1.3\pm1.4\%$ above Hungarian ($p=0.055$ and
$0.077$), again not rejected. Among the 46 secondary comparisons one survives Holm's
procedure: the service time of \sname\ against greedy assignment with $\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}=4$ on the
warehouse, $-1.2\pm0.6\%$. Four more exclude zero at the nominal level only. We do not
build a claim on a single secondary service-time difference of an optional arm. No run of any
arm had an executed conflict, an illegal move, a stall or a depletion.

The null result therefore also holds where about half of the calls are real
assignment problems, with half-widths of 1.1 and 1.5\% on the primary comparisons. That
weakens the explanation by degenerate calls alone: the inertness of the
pickup-leg cost is not only an artefact of how few calls it can change. It is still
not an equivalence test, and it still concerns the pickup-leg cost under the
dock-capacity gate.
