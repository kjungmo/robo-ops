\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
\input{figures/overview}

An operator console for a fleet of autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) shows the
same few things on every screen: which robot is moving where, which task it
carries, how much battery it has and which zone or dock it is heading for.
Behind those screens sits a fleet management system (FMS) that decides all of
it, continuously: new tasks arrive, idle robots must be matched to them,
every robot needs a path that does not collide with any other, and robots
must be sent to chargers before they strand. Each of these problems is well
studied on its own---multi-robot task allocation
\citep{gerkey2004formal,korsah2013comprehensive}, multi-agent path finding
(MAPF) \citep{stern2019mapf,sharon2015cbs} and its lifelong
pickup-and-delivery variant \citep{ma2017lifelong,li2021rhcr}---but a
deployable FMS has to run them together, at every tick, with hard safety
constraints.

The interface between allocation and path finding is often simplified.
Assignment costs are commonly free-space distances
\citep{ma2017lifelong,liu2019task}, which ignore the traffic that the planner
already knows about. Coupling the two has been studied at several levels of
fidelity. Exact methods (CBM, \citealp{ma2016optimal}; CBS-TA,
\citealp{honig2018cbsta}) solve one-shot instances, and \citet{fan2025joint}
extend a CBS-based joint search to a continuous task stream by re-solving it
when the task set changes. In online pickup and delivery, token passing with
task swaps lets a robot take over a task whose pickup it reaches earlier
\citep{ma2017lifelong}; \citet{shimokawa2022improvement} estimate pickup
times to choose the robot in token passing; RMCA prices assignments with
collision-free paths from prioritized planning and improves them by large
neighbourhood search \citep{chen2021integrated}; LNS-wPBS combines
Hungarian-based insertion and large neighbourhood search with windowed
planning \citep{xu2022multigoal}; and, in concurrent work, Detour-Delta scores
tasks by the extra delivery time that committed space-time reservations
impose \citep{hirayama2026detour}. These online methods, an evaluation of
task-assignment strategies for capacitated agents in lifelong pickup and
delivery \citep{cilden2025task}, and a simulation study of online assignment
on warehouse scenarios from a deployed logistics operator
\citep{zhu2026combined},
find that the choice of assignment rule changes performance. Batteries
\citep{kudo2024anytime,tang2026energy} and docks with finite buffers
\citep{zang2026handover} have also been added to lifelong pickup and
delivery. What we have not found measured is a narrower question: in a loop
that also has batteries, loading dwell and per-dock limits on the tasks in
flight, does replacing free-space robot-to-pickup costs by reservation-aware
arrival times at the pickup change throughput?

To answer that question with a system rather than a thought experiment, we
introduce \emph{\sname} (Path-Aware Coupled Tasking), an end-to-end,
framework-free lifelong fleet manager for grid warehouses
(\cref{fig:overview}). \sname\ replans the fleet every $h$ ticks over a window
of $w$ ticks, as in RHCR \citep{li2021rhcr}, with windowed space-time A*
\citep{silver2005cooperative} under a reservation table and prioritized
planning \citep{erdmann1987multiple}. Its coupling is a deliberately
cheap one that uses real plans: the cost of assigning robot $r$ to task
$\tau$ is the arrival time that the same planner reports for $r$'s trip to
the pickup against the reservations of the robots that are already busy, and
the Hungarian method \citep{kuhn1955hungarian} turns those costs into an
assignment that is optimal for that cost matrix. Unlike RMCA and
Detour-Delta, the cost prices only the empty trip to the pickup, and it does
not price the interactions among the robots assigned in the same epoch.
Around the coupling sit the pieces that make a lifelong
loop robust: a hold cascade that converts planner failures into consistent
rests (a variant of the fail policies of \citet{morag2023adapting}, in which
the robots whose reservations cross a held cell are re-planned rather than
made to stay in place), token-passing endpoint capacities
\citep{ma2017lifelong} that keep dock
queues short, a two-threshold charging policy with capacity-constrained slot
assignment, and an optional windowed Conflict-Based Search backend
\citep{sharon2015cbs}.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{A tested lifelong FMS stack}: grid map model with benchmark
  layouts (one generated from the zone and dock labels of an operator-console
  wireframe), Hungarian and greedy allocation, windowed space-time A*,
  prioritized planning with a hold cascade, windowed CBS, charging, and a
  seeded discrete-time simulator; conflict-freeness of every executed step
  is proved by the standard reservation-table argument extended to holds, the
  optimality of the Hungarian step for its cost matrix is restated from the
  assignment literature, and a test suite checks the
  Hungarian and CBS implementations against exhaustive search and the planners
  against property tests. None of these components is new on its own.
  \item \emph{Reservation-aware pickup costs}: Hungarian assignment on
  arrival times at the pickup from the windowed planner, with a
  congestion-proxy middle ground, a battery term and candidate pruning,
  designed so that the coupling adds only a bounded number of windowed A*
  calls per epoch. Pricing assignments with planned paths or estimated pickup times is not
  new \citep{shimokawa2022improvement,chen2021integrated,hirayama2026detour}; this is a cheaper instance
  that prices only the pickup leg.
  \item \emph{A controlled study of what moves a fleet}: 420 seeded runs on
  three layouts sweep allocation cost model, planner backend, fleet size,
  load regime, window, replanning period, dock capacity and battery
  parameters; a second round of 6{,}180 runs adds a 60-seed safety stress
  test checked by an independent trajectory auditor, an ablation of the hold
  cascade and of the priorities, token-passing baselines and four regimes
  chosen to favour the coupling and committed before their results. Every number traces to a
  committed result file.
\end{itemize}

The study's main result is negative and narrow: with dock capacity limiting
which tasks can be assigned, in every one of
twelve layout/fleet/load cells the four allocation cost models---greedy,
Hungarian, congestion proxy and reservation-aware pickup cost---differ by at most 2.9\% in
mean throughput, and 35 of 36 seed-paired 95\% intervals against Hungarian
contain zero (\cref{tab:main,tab:paired}). With five seeds the interval
bounds reach about 7\% in the noisiest cell and about 4\% under the Poisson
load, so the study detects no large effect but cannot resolve small ones. It
was not designed as an equivalence test and fixes no equivalence margin, so
it does not show that the effect is zero; nor does it test costs that price
the loaded leg, the dock queue or the interactions among the robots being
assigned. What does move the fleet is elsewhere: raising the
per-dock in-flight capacity from 2 to 3 raises throughput by 26--29\%,
shortening the planning window from 20 to 10 ticks raises it by 7\% while
cutting planner time by $3.3\times$, and windowed CBS reduces makespan by up to
8\% on small fleets (\cref{tab:ablation,tab:planner}); the seed-paired 95\%
intervals of all three exclude zero. In the four regimes we chose to
favour the coupling, \sname\ shows no resolved gain except where every method
strands robots, and there its higher throughput mostly reflects fewer stalled runs
(\cref{subsec:coupling}).
Token passing and token passing with task swaps \citep{ma2017lifelong}
deliver 16--66\% less throughput than our loop at its default dock
capacity, but their endpoint rule allows one robot in flight
per dock; at that capacity our loop and token passing with task swaps are
within $-2.5$ to $+1.6\%$ of each other (\cref{subsec:baselines}), so the
gap to token passing with task swaps measures dock capacity, not our
allocation or planner.
Throughout, the executed plans of 60.4 million audited robot-steps contain no
vertex or swap conflict, as the construction guarantees. Progress and charge
are not guaranteed: with normal batteries no robot depletes and every run
delivers all of its tasks, but with doubled drain the charging policy
strands robots once its slots saturate (\cref{subsec:main}), and we prove
only a conditional reserve bound (\cref{prop:charge}). We release the
implementation inside the operator-console codebase it was developed in,
together with
all results.
