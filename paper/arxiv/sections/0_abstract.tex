\begin{abstract}
Warehouse fleet managers must allocate a stream of pickup-and-delivery
tasks, plan collision-free paths and manage batteries, continuously. Prior
work couples allocation and path finding exactly for one-shot instances, and
approximately online by pricing tasks with planned paths or reservation
delays. We introduce \emph{\sname}, a lifelong fleet manager for grid
warehouses that uses a cheap member of that family: assignment costs are
arrival times at the pickup of the same windowed space-time A* that plans the
fleet, evaluated against busy robots' reservations, fed to a Hungarian
assignment. A hold cascade, a variant of existing fail policies, keeps
prioritized planning conflict-free, and token-passing-style endpoint
capacities bound dock queues. We prove that every executed step is vertex-
and swap-conflict-free; progress and battery safety are not guaranteed,
so we measure both. In
a seeded simulator on four synthetic layouts, an independent auditor finds
no executed conflict in 4{,}740 runs with 60.4 million robot-steps; with
normal batteries no robot depletes, but with doubled drain the charging
policy strands robots in two thirds of runs. The coupling study is a
negative and weak result: greedy, Hungarian, congestion-proxy and
reservation-aware pickup costs stay within 2.9\% of each other in mean
throughput in all twelve layout/fleet/load cells, but counters show
that the reservation-aware cost changes the matching in only 1--14\% of
the allocations, since most pickups lie beyond its window.
The study detects no large effect, is not an equivalence test, and omits
costs that price the whole trip or the dock queue. With the same
one-robot-per-dock limit, token passing with task swaps is within $-10.7$ to
$+3.7\%$ of our loop, depending on its idle rule. Dock capacity,
the planning window and windowed conflict-based search are the levers that
move the fleet. Code:
\url{https://github.com/kjungmo/robo-ops}
\end{abstract}
